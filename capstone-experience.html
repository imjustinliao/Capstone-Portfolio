Capstone Experience

Most dating apps today feel like work. We built Serene to take the opposite approach. This page is the capstone log: research, MVP scope, design, implementation, integration, and TestFlight beta. Justin led product, design, and frontend. Andriy led backend and systems. We coordinated weekly and connected the frontend and backend together near the end of the quarter.

Research and Stakeholder Insights

We ran stakeholder interviews, surveys, and product research on apps like Hinge and Coffee Meets Bagel. Conversations fade. People overthink their profiles. Apps are designed around engagement, not meeting in real life. We used that to position Serene: college students 18 to 26, real profiles, meet IRL.

The summary captured why people leave other apps: profiles that feel too polished and chats that fizzle out. We used it to commit to real photos in onboarding and to meeting in person as the main goal. We kept these themes in mind when we made design and scope decisions.

We compared incumbents on authenticity, location, and college focus. The table justified our bet on real photos and proximity-based discovery instead of copying swipe mechanics.

MVP Scope and Product Decisions

Our explicit goals: design and prototype the experience, build an iOS app, validate with real college students. We used a waitlist strategy and the dubmatch.com waitlist site. We mapped the full user flow and kept scope tight: profile (Polaroid + basics), discovery, match, one IRL meet with safety guardrails.

The requirements doc was the single source of truth for what we shipped. We cut in-app chat depth and kept the 30-minute meeting window and QR check-in from the start.

We mapped the full user flow so design and backend stayed aligned. Location and notification permissions were explicit at each step. Andriy used it to align API endpoints and safety checks.

UI/UX Design in Figma (Justin)

Justin led the Figma UI/UX design: short steps so the flow never felt overwhelming, clear rationale for location and notifications, Polaroids, and a minimal interface designed to build trust without extra friction.

Early wireframes had too many steps. The refined version collapsed explanation and capture into a single sequence. The comparison shows the pivot from explain everything to show once, then capture.

This flow explained why location and notifications were needed and placed the Polaroid step so users understood why a real photo mattered. The flow was designed to build trust. We kept it in the SwiftUI build with minimal change.

iOS Frontend Implementation (SwiftUI) (Justin)

Justin led the SwiftUI frontend and Apple Maps integration. The app used reusable UI patterns, stayed iOS-native, and followed Apple HCI guidance (including liquid glass where it fit). We wired it to the backend API.

Navigation and state were structured around discovery, profile, and meet flow. The separation made it easier to plug in API calls and location updates. We refactored once when the meet flow gained states like pending, confirmed, expired.

Apple Maps showed nearby users (within the backend radius) and supported the meet IRL step with directions. The team balanced privacy (no persistent precise location) with enough detail for who is around and meet confirmation.

Backend Architecture and Data (Flask + Supabase) (Andriy)

Andriy led the Flask API and Supabase data model. The architecture supported auth, profiles, matches, and meeting/safety data. We aligned on request/response shapes early so the iOS client could integrate without guesswork.

The diagram showed which endpoints handled discovery, matching, and meet lifecycle. We added safety-related endpoints after locking the core flows and updated the diagram to match.

The schema made match and meeting state explicit (time window, check-in status). It supported the proximity engine and the safety flow. We kept migrations minimal.

Matchmaking, Proximity Engine, and Safety (Backend + Product)

Andriy led the proximity engine (Haversine) and match score logic with dealbreakers. Product and backend agreed on radius and refresh behavior. Safety: 30-minute meeting window, QR check-in, and expiration so both sides have a clear, bounded frame.

Haversine gave us who is nearby at campus scale without heavy GIS. We tuned the radius and how often the client refreshed. The diagram is the reference for the logic.

The flow made the meeting window and check-in mandatory so both parties opt in. Expiration cleared stale pending states. We kept the description factual and did not over-claim.
