Capstone Experience

Most dating apps today feel like work. We built Serene to take the opposite approach. This page shows how it evolved from research through MVP scope, design, implementation, integration, and TestFlight beta. I (Justin) handled product, design, and frontend; Andriy handled backend. We coordinated weekly and integrated the iOS app with the API near the end of the quarter.

Research and Stakeholder Insights

We started with surveys and interviews. Swiping rarely turns into real dates; profiles are too perfect, not real; chats lose momentum. The insights drove our positioning: authenticity, IRL meetups, and college students ages 18 to 26.

The summary surfaced fatigue with curated profiles and endless chat. We used it to lock in Polaroid-first onboarding and “meet IRL” as the north star. Later design and scope decisions referred back to these themes.

The table compared incumbents on authenticity, location, and college focus. It justified our bet on real photos and proximity-based discovery instead of duplicating swipe mechanics.

MVP Scope and Product Decisions

We defined MVP requirements and a user flow map so design and backend stayed aligned. Scope stayed tight: profile (Polaroid + basics), discovery, lightweight match, and a clear path to one IRL meet with safety guardrails.

The requirements doc became the single source of truth for what we would ship in the capstone window. We cut “nice-to-haves” like in-app chat depth and kept the 30-minute meeting window and QR check-in from the start.

The flow made location and notification permissions explicit so we could design and implement them consistently. Andriy used it to align API endpoints and safety checks with each step.

UI/UX Design in Figma (Justin)

I designed the app in Figma from wireframes to higher-fidelity screens. The main tension was onboarding: we needed Polaroid and permissions without feeling heavy. Several iterations simplified the trust flow.

Early wireframes had too many steps; the refined version collapsed explanation and capture into a single, clear sequence. The comparison documents the pivot from “explain everything” to “show once, then capture.”

This flow tied permission requests to clear rationale (e.g., “see who’s nearby”) and placed the Polaroid step after basics so users understood why a real photo mattered. We kept it in the SwiftUI build with minimal change.

iOS Frontend Implementation (SwiftUI) (Justin)

I implemented the iOS app in SwiftUI and wired it to the backend API. I structured navigation and state around discovery, profile, and meet flow. Apple Maps integration handled “nearby” and map guidance for meetups.

A clear separation between discovery, profile, and meet views made it easier to plug in API calls and location updates. We refactored once mid-quarter when the meet flow gained additional states (e.g., pending, confirmed, expired).

Maps showed nearby users (within backend-defined radius) and supported the “meet IRL” step with directions. We had to balance privacy (no persistent precise location) with enough detail for “who’s around” and meet confirmation.

Backend Architecture and Data (Flask + Supabase) (Andriy)

Andriy built the REST API in Python/Flask and used Supabase/Postgres for persistence. The architecture supported auth, profiles, matches, and meeting/safety data. We aligned on request/response shapes early so the iOS client could integrate without guesswork.

The diagram clarified which endpoints handled discovery, matching, and meet lifecycle. We added a small set of safety-related endpoints after locking the core flows, and we updated the diagram to reflect that.

The schema made match and meeting state explicit (e.g., time window, check-in status). That supported both the proximity engine and the safety flow without ad-hoc fields. We kept migrations minimal during the quarter.

Matchmaking, Proximity Engine, and Safety (Backend + Product)

The proximity engine used Haversine distance to surface “who’s nearby”; product and backend agreed on radius and refresh behavior. Safety relied on a 30-minute meeting window, QR check-in, and expiration so both sides had a clear, bounded frame.

Haversine gave us “good enough” distance for campus-scale discovery without heavy GIS. We tuned the radius and how often the client refreshed so the list felt live but not noisy. The diagram stayed as the reference for any future tweaks.

The flow made the meeting window and check-in mandatory in the product logic so both parties had to opt in. Expiration prevented stale “pending” states. We kept the description factual and avoided over-claiming what the app could guarantee.

Integration, TestFlight, and Beta Testing

We integrated the iOS app with the Flask API, ran through a short checklist (auth, discovery, match, meet, safety), and shipped to TestFlight for beta testers. Feedback drove small copy and flow tweaks before we closed the capstone.

The checklist ensured we didn’t ship with broken auth or missing error handling on key screens. TestFlight gave us a repeatable path to get builds to testers without App Store review during development.

Testers confirmed that Polaroid and the meet flow felt different from typical apps; confusion centered on when to enable location and what “nearby” meant. We adjusted copy and one onboarding step in response. The notes document what we changed and why.

What I learned: applied learning came from doing the full loop—research to scope to design to implementation to integration and beta. The biggest growth was in making decisions under constraints (time, scope, two people) and iterating based on feedback instead of assumptions. Aligning with Andriy on API and safety early prevented rework later; these artifacts are the evidence of that progression.
